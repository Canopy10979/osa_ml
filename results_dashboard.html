Can a machine detect sleep apnea?

Five datasets were run through the same pipeline. Three produced real answers, and the newest one is the first that pinpoints individual apneas rather than just recognizing which patients are sick. This dashboard shows what each dataset could and could not support, and which physiological signals actually drove the predictions.

Share of skill that is genuine event detection The two heartbeat datasets get 40–59% of their apparent skill from learning who the patient is and riding their overall severity. Here that figure is only 7%. The reason is sensor access: blood oxygen drops during an apnea, while heart-rate effects are a diffuse downstream echo that partly encodes the person.

The top nine features are all blood-oxygen measures When you stop breathing, your blood oxygen falls and then recovers with the gasp. That dip is the direct physical consequence of an apnea, and it is what this model reads. Worth contrasting with the legacy cross_dataset analysis below, which gave blood oxygen an importance of 0.0019 and still called itself an OSA model.

Can it tell who has OSA?

Ranking patients by severity predicted vs true AHI

Yes, with a caveat about size Predicted severity tracks the clinical score at r = 0.951, and a leave-one-patient-out test — where each patient's own data never sets their threshold — gets 84.7% balanced accuracy. But this is 25 people. The perfect specificity (11/11 healthy correctly cleared) has a wide margin of error and should not be read as "never wrong."
It fills the gap the other datasets left 16 of these 25 patients sit in the AHI 5–25 range — the mild-to-moderate band that is hardest to diagnose, and which apnea_hrv excludes entirely by design. This is the cohort that tests the cases that actually matter.

What this means in plain terms Every apnea ends in a gasp and a brief arousal, which spikes the heart rate. Repeated all night, that produces a slow rhythmic surge-and-settle pattern roughly every 25–100 seconds. Here that cycling is the top signal (0.806 on its own) — the four strongest features are all versions of it. The breathing-linked measure that dominated the ECG dataset is present too, but second.
Why the ECG dataset “disagreed” — it didn’t The apnea_ecg report recorded, as a finding, that this cycling signal didn’t matter. That was a bug. Its CVHR features are identically zero for all 13,286 minutes: the code discards a frequency band unless more than two FFT bins land inside it, and at that pipeline’s resolution exactly two did. The signal was never measured, not measured-and-absent.

Essentially zero transfer loss A model that has never seen a single PhysioNet recording detects apnea minutes in it as well as a model trained on that data directly — a gap of 0.001. Different hardware, different country, and an apnea rate of 22% vs 49%. This is the best evidence in the whole project that the signal is real physiology rather than something memorised from one dataset.

The honest catch — same caveat as the ECG dataset, larger

Excellent screener, weaker event detector About 59% of the model’s skill above chance comes from recognizing who the person is rather than which specific minute is an apnea. For screening — the actual clinical use — that is fine, and it is exactly why the patient-vs-control number is so strong. It does mean the per-minute figure overstates true event detection.
The missing middle This cohort is deliberately gapped: controls are all below AHI 5, patients all above AHI 25, and nobody sits in between. That 5–25 band is precisely the mild-to-moderate range that is hardest to diagnose and most common in practice. Nothing here says how the model performs on those patients.

Why this is trustworthy Scrambling the answers collapsed performance to chance (0.489) — the model wasn’t memorizing leaked information. Re-running with different random seeds moved the score by less than 0.005. The gap between 0.838 and 0.489 is large and real.

The headline correlation

r_hf breathing-linked heart rate swing
−0.46
−1.0 (predicts apnea)0+1.0 (predicts normal)
What r_hf means in plain terms Your heart normally speeds up and slows down in rhythm with your breathing. In healthy sleep that fluctuation is strong. During apnea minutes it collapses. That single measurement alone reaches 0.793 — nearly the whole model — and its negative sign points exactly the direction physiology says it should. This is a genuine biological signal, not a statistical artifact.

5 · cross_dataset

⚠️ Was mislabeled — now fixed

This folder once reported 97% accuracy at detecting OSA. It was not detecting OSA. The mislabeling has now been traced, corrected, and the analysis re-run with the real apnea labels — and the honest answer is that this data cannot support an OSA claim.

What was wrong

It was predicting “asleep vs awake,” labeled as “has OSA vs doesn’t” The target column was Sleep_Label, and the code that produced it says so outright: “0=awake, 1=asleep.” Because 80% of a night is spent asleep, always guessing “asleep” already scores about 80% — which is where the impressive-looking 97% came from. The giveaway was that blood oxygen, the defining sign of apnea, was given an importance of 0.0019.

The fix — we found out whose night this is

It is UCDDB011, and the real apnea labels exist The recording was unattributed. Its start time (22:47:44) and 8.9-hour length match patient UCDDB011 in dataset_ucddb_v2 — start 22:47:38, recorded AHI 8. So the expert's actual apnea list for that night could be attached in place of the sleep labels. Alignment checks out: the re-derived rate is 7.8 events/hour against a recorded AHI of 8.

A negative result, reported as one With the correct labels the best model barely clears chance, and both tree models refuse to predict a single apnea. The 97% headline does not survive correction. Feature importance does at least move the right way — airflow reduction becomes the top signal and blood oxygen rises from 0.0019 to 23% of total importance — so the signal is faintly present. There is just not enough of it here.

Why it fails — and why that is reassuring

The same patient is detected at 0.928 in the full dataset This is not a contradiction of dataset_ucddb_v2 — it shows what is lost when one night is stripped down. Here: one patient with mild disease (AHI 8), only 56 apnea epochs to learn from, and just three low-rate channels (1 Hz heart rate and oxygen, 2 Hz airflow) with no chest or belly effort belts. The full study keeps 8 Hz oxygen and airflow plus effort, across 25 patients.

Bottom line: dataset_ucddb_v2 gives the cleanest result — with full sleep-lab sensors it pinpoints individual apneas (AUC 0.928) rather than just recognizing which patients are sick, and only ~7% of its skill comes from patient identity. dataset_apnea_hrv is the best screener: from heartbeat alone it separates 37 patients from 40 healthy controls at AUC 0.937, and its findings transfer to a completely different hospital with almost no loss. dataset_apnea_ecg confirms the heartbeat signal but has only 2 healthy controls, so it cannot rule OSA out. The remaining two datasets are undefined or measuring the wrong thing. Nothing here is a validated clinical tool: the largest cohort is 77 people, and the heartbeat models lean heavily on recognizing the person.
